Recall the repair ILP objective $F:\Z^n\rightarrow\R$ defined by
$$
	F(d) = \|w\odot(Bd-r)\|_1+\lambda\|v\odot d\|_1
$$
where
$$
	r = \lceil x^+\rceil - \lceil x^-\rceil - B(\lceil y^+\rceil - \lceil y^-\rceil) - c.
$$


Recall that we found that
$$
	\lambda\|v\odot d\|_1\le \|w\odot r\|_1,
$$
i.e.,
$$
	\lambda\sum_{i=1}^nv_i|d_i|\le|w\odot r\|_1.
$$
Notice then that
$$
	\lambda\|v\odot d\|_1 \ge \lambda\sum_{i=1}^n|d_i|\min_jv_i \Longrightarrow \|d\|_1\le\frac{\|w\odot r\|_1}{\lambda\min_j v_j}\Longrightarrow |d_i|\le\frac{\|w\odot r\|_1}{n\lambda\min_j v_j},
$$
i.e., $d$ can be restricted to a hyperrectangle.

**Proposition:** Let $z_{LP}$ be the optimal objective value to the LP relaxation of the flat norm and $z$ the objective value obtained by taking the ceiling of $y^\pm$ and recomputing $x$. Then it follows that
$$
	0 \le z - z_{LP} \le w^\top|B(\delta^+-\delta^-)|+\lambda v^\top(\delta^++\delta^-).
$$

*Proof.* Let $\delta^\pm=\lceil y^\pm\rceil - y^\pm$ and notice that $0<\delta^\pm<1$. Moreover,
$$
	|(B(\delta^+-\delta^-))_i|=\left|\sum_jB_{ij}(\delta_j^+-\delta_j^-)\right| \le \sum_j|B_{ij}||\delta_j^+-\delta_j^-| = \sum_j|B_{ij}|(\delta_j^++\delta_j^-).
$$
Therefore, it follows that
$$
\begin{align*}
	z - z_{LP} &= \left[w^\top|c+B(\lceil y^+\rceil-\lceil y^-\rceil)| + \lambda v^\top(\lceil y^+\rceil + \lceil y^-\rceil)\right] - \left[w^\top|c+B(y^+-y^-)| + \lambda v^\top(y^+ + y^-)\right] \\
	&= w^\top\left[|c+B(\lceil y^+\rceil-\lceil y^-\rceil)| -|c+B( y^+- y^-)| \right]+\lambda v^\top(\delta^++\delta^-) \\
	&\le w^\top|B(\delta^+-\delta^-)|+\lambda v^\top(\delta^++\delta^-) \\
	&\le w^\top|B|(\delta^++\delta^-)+\lambda v^\top(\delta^++\delta^-) \\
	&= (|B|^\top w+\lambda v)^\top(\delta^++\delta^-).~\square
\end{align*}
$$

**Theorem 1:** If $B=\partial_p$ is totally bimodular, then the flat norm is solvable in strongly polynomial time.

*Proof.* According to Artmann et al., an ILP of the form $\max\{c^\top x:Ax\le b,~x\in\Z^n\}$ is solveable in strongly polynomial time if every $n\times n$ submatrix of $A$ has absolute determinant at most 2. Write $A=\begin{bmatrix} I & A'\end{bmatrix}$ with $A'=\begin{bmatrix} -I & -B & B\end{bmatrix}$ and set $u=(x^-,y^+,y^-)\in\Z^d$ where $d=m+2n$. We can recover $x^+$ by $x^+=b-A'u$. In order to ensure that $x^+\ge0$, we need that $A'u\le b$. As a result, we can rewrite the flat norm ILP as
$$
\begin{align*}
	&\text{minimize} & w^\top b +(\begin{bmatrix}w^\top&\lambda v^\top & \lambda v^\top\end{bmatrix}-w^\top A')u \\
	&\text{subject to} & \begin{bmatrix} A'\\-I_d\end{bmatrix}u\le\begin{bmatrix} b\\0\end{bmatrix} \\
	&& u\in\Z^d.
\end{align*}
$$
Denote by $G$ the constraint matrix, which has full column rank. Consider a $d\times d$ submatrix $G'$ by choosing rows $Z$ of $A'$ and rows $S$ of $-I_d$ where $|Z|+|S|=d$ and $Z\ne\emptyset$. We claim that
$$
	\det G' = \pm\det A'[Z,[d]\setminus S].
$$
Indeed, proceed via induction on $d$. If $d=3$, then it follows that
$$
	G = \begin{bmatrix}
		-1 & -\sigma & \sigma \\
		-1 & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & -1 & 0 \\
		0 & 0 & -1
	\end{bmatrix}
$$
for some $\sigma\in\Z$. The result follows by expanding along rows of $S$ until we get a $1\times 1$ matrix. Consider then a $(d+1)\times (d+1)$ submatrix $G'$ of $G$. Select any identity row to expand upon, resulting in a $d\times d$ submatrix $H$. Thus, it follows by the induction hypothesis that
$$
	\det G' = \pm\det H = \pm\det A'[Z,[d]\setminus S].
$$
A similar argument may be done on the columns of $A'$. Thus, we get that
$$
	\max|\text{$m\times m$ minor of $G$}|=\max|\text{$m\times m$ minor of $A$}| = \max_{Z,T}|\det B[Z,T]|.
$$
Therefore, the flat norm is solveable in strongly polynomial time if $B$ is totally bimodular. $\square$

# Quick Recap of Relative Homology

Consider a subcomplex $A\subset X$ of a simplicial complex $X$. Then $C_p(A)$ is the subgroup of $C_p(X)$ consisting of chains that live in $A$. We define the quotient chain group
$$
	C_p(X,A) = C_p(X) / C_p(A)
$$
which is the free abelian group generated by the $p$-simplices of $X$ that are not in $A$. Because $\partial C_p(A)\subseteq C_{p-1}(A)$, it follows that
$$
	\cdots\rightarrow C_{p}(X,A)\xrightarrow{\partial_p}C_{p-1}(X,A)\rightarrow\cdots
$$
forms a chain complex. Thus, we can define 
$$
	H_p(X,A) = \textup{Ker}(\partial_p)/\textup{Im}(\partial_{p+1})
$$
to be the $p$-th relative homology group of the pair $(X,A)$. More concretely, a class in $H_p(X,A)$ is represented by a chain $c\in C_p(X)$ with $\partial c\in C_{p-1}(A)$ and is identified with $c'\in C_p(X)$ if
$$
	c - c' = \partial b + a
$$
for some $b\in C_{p+1}(X)$ and $a\in C_p(A)$. The relative homology is directly related to reduced homology:
$$
	H_p(X,A)\cong \tilde{H}_p(X/A).
$$

One may construct the short exact sequence
$$
	0 \rightarrow C_p(A) \xrightarrow{i} C_p(X)\xrightarrow{j}C_p(X,A)\rightarrow0
$$
where $i$ is given by inclusion and $j$ is given by quotient. Using the snake lemma, this can be extended into a long exact sequence of homology groups:
$$
	\cdots\rightarrow H_p(A)\xrightarrow{i_*}H_p(X)\xrightarrow{j_*}H_p(X,A)\xrightarrow{\partial_p}H_{p-1}(A)\xrightarrow{i_*}H_{p-1}(X)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow H_0(X,A)\rightarrow0.
$$
The exact sequences are often used to determine the relative homology groups.

A commonly used result is that of excision. If $Z\subseteq A\subseteq X$ such that $Z$ is a subspace of $A$, then
$$
	H_p(X-Z,A-Z) \cong H_p(X,A).
$$
In simpler terms, if the closure of $Z$ is contained in the interior of $A$, then one may cut out $U$ without changing the relative homology.

**Example:** Consider $S^{n-1}\subset D^n$ with $n\ge1$. Because $D^n$ is contractible we get that $\tilde{H}_k(D^n)=0$ for all $k$ which gives that
$$
	0 = \tilde{H}_k(D^n)\rightarrow H_k(D^n,S^{n-1})\xrightarrow{\partial} \tilde{H}_{k-1}(S^{n-1})\rightarrow \tilde{H}_{k-1}(D^n)=0.
$$
Because this forms a short exact sequence, $\partial$ is an isomorphism. Hence
$$
	H_k(D^n,S^{n-1})\cong \tilde{H}_{k-1}(S^{n-1}) = \begin{cases}
		\Z & k = n \\
		0 & \text{otherwise.}
	\end{cases}
$$

# Relative Homology and Total Bimodularity

**Claim:** For submatrix $B[Z,T]$ of $B=[\partial_{p+1}]$ with rows $Z$ and columns $T$, define
$$
\begin{align*}
	L &= \text{Cl}(T)= \{\sigma\in K:\exists\tau\in T,~\sigma\subseteq\tau\} \\
	L_0 &= \text{Cl}(L^{(p)}\setminus Z)=\{\sigma\in K:\exists\tau\in L^{(p)}\setminus Z,~\sigma\subseteq\tau\}.
\end{align*}
$$
Then $B[Z,T]$ is the matrix representation of $\partial_{p+1}':C_{p+1}(L,L_0)\rightarrow C_p(L,L_0)$ where $C_*(L,L_0):=C_*(L)/C_*(L_0)$.

We are thus interested in the homology
$$
	H_p(L,L_0) = \textup{Ker}(\partial_{p}')/\textup{Im}(\partial_{p+1}')\cong\Z^{m-r-s}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\Z/\alpha_i
$$
where $m=|Z|$, $r=\textup{rank}(\partial_{p+1}')$, $s=\textup{rank}(\partial_p')$ and $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_r$ are the invariant factors given by the Smith normal form of $[\partial_{p+1}']$.
 
**Lemma 2:** $B=[\partial_{p+1}]$ is totally bimodular if and only if, for every pair of pure subcomplexes $L_0\subseteq L\subseteq K$ of dimensions $p$ and $p+1$, the torsion subgroup of $H_p(L,L_0)$ has order at most 2.

*Proof.*

($\Rightarrow$): Consider an arbitrary pair $(L,L_0)$ and set $T=L^{(p+1)}$ and $Z=L^{(p)}\setminus L_0$. Then $H_p(L,L_0)$ has torsion of order
$$
	\prod_{i=1}^r\alpha_i = d_r(\partial_{p+1}') = \textup{gcd}\{r\times r\text{ minors of $B[Z,T]$}\} \le 2.
$$
($\Leftarrow$): Let $B[Z,T]$ be a square submatrix of $B$. Then it follows that 
$$
	|\det B[Z,T]|=\begin{cases}
		\prod_{i=1}^r\alpha_i & r=|Z| \\
		0 & r < |Z|.
	\end{cases}
$$
Because the torsion subgroup of $H_p(L,L_0)$ has order at most 2, it follows that $|\det B[Z,T]|\le2$. $\square$

**Theorem 4:** If $K$ is a 2-complex that embeds into $\R P^2$, then $B=[\partial_2]$ is totally bimodular.

*Proof.* Assume for the sake of contradiction that $K$ has at least two disjoint Mobius strips $N_1, N_2$. Let $L=N_1\cup N_2$ and $E$ be the closure such that $\R P^2=L\cup E$. Because $N_1$ and $N_2$ are disjoint, it follows that $L\cap E=\partial N_1\cup\partial N_2$. Then the Mayer-Vietoris sequence contains
$$
	H_2(\R P^2)\rightarrow H_1(L\cap E)\xrightarrow{\varphi}  H_1(L)\oplus H_1(E) \xrightarrow{\psi} H_1(\R P^2).
$$
Due to exactness, we get that $\textup{ker}\psi=\textup{im}\varphi$ which implies by the fundamental theorem of homomorphisms that there is a unique injective homomorphism
$$
	h:H_1(L)\oplus H_1(E)/\textup{im}\varphi\rightarrow H_1(\R P^2) = \Z/2\Z.
$$
Notice that the map $\varphi$ is of the form $(i_*,j_*)$ where $i:L\cap E\hookrightarrow L$ and $j:L\cap E\hookrightarrow E$. Because $L\cap E=\partial N_1\cup\partial N_2$, an element $(x,y)\in H_1(L\cap E)=\Z\oplus \Z$ implies wrapping $x$ times around $\partial N_1$ and $y$ times around $\partial N_2$. Thus a loop around $\partial N_1$ is recorded twice, once in $L$ and once in $E$. Thus,
$$
\begin{align*}
	i_*(1,0) = \pm2~\text { and }~i_*(0,1) = \pm 2.
\end{align*}
$$
So $\varphi(x,y)=(\pm2,\pm2,e)$ for some $e\in H_1(E)$. Consider then the map
$$
	\pi:\Z\oplus\Z\oplus H_1(E)\rightarrow(\Z/2\Z)^2,~(a,b,e)\mapsto(a\bmod 2,b\bmod 2)
$$
which has $\textup{ker}\pi=\textup{im}\varphi$. Then $\pi$ induces an injective map 
$$
	g:\Z\oplus\Z\oplus H_1(E)/\text{im}\varphi\rightarrow (\Z/2\Z)^2
$$
such that $\pi=g\circ\varphi$. Because $\pi$ is surjective, it follows that $g$ is an isomorphism. Thus, $H_1(L)\oplus H_1(E)\cong(\Z/2\Z)^2$, which contradicts $h$. $\square$

**Proposition 5:** If $K$ has two disjoint pure subcomplexes with relative torsion, then $[\partial_{p+1}]$ is not totally bimodular.

*Proof.* Let $A_0\subseteq A\subseteq K$ and $B_0\subseteq B\subseteq K$ be two disjoint pairs of pure subcomplexes (i.e. $A\cap B=\emptyset$) such that $H_p(A,A_0)\cong\Z/2\Z$ and $H_p(B,B_0)\cong\Z/2\Z$. Consider then $L_0=A_0\cup B_0$ and $L=A\cup B$. Then the Mayer-Vietoris sequence is
$$
	0 = H_p(A\cap B, A_0\cap B_0)\rightarrow H_p(A,A_0)\oplus H_p(B,B_0)\rightarrow H_p(L,L_0)\rightarrow H_{p-1}(A\cap B,A_0\cap B_0)=0.
$$
Because the sequence is exact, it follows that
$$
	H_p(L,L_0) \cong H_p(A,A_0)\oplus H_p(B,B_0)\cong\Z/2\Z\oplus\Z/2\Z.
$$
Therefore, the square submatrix corresponding to $(L,L_0)$ has determinant 4. $\square$
