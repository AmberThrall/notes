Consider the following linear program: 
$$
\begin{align*}
	&\textup{minimize} & \sum_{i=1}^m w_i(x_i^++x_i^-) + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^n v_j(y_j^++y_j^-) \\
	&\textup{subject to} & x^+ - x^- - By = c \\
	&& x^+,x^-,y^+,y^-\ge0
\end{align*}
$$
where $I$ is the $m\times m$-identity matrix, $B$ is the boundary matrix for $\partial:C_p(K)\rightarrow C_{p-1}(K)$} for some simplicial complex $K$ embedded in $\R^{p+1}$, $w$ and $v$ are real non-negative vectors, $\lambda\in\R$ with $\lambda\ge0$, and $c$ is a $(p-1)$-chain. In the majority of cases, the optimal solution $(x^*,y^*)$ is integral; even if the boundary matrix $B$ is not totally unimodular. However, I was able to find one instance by playing with the weights in which simplex method gave a fractional solution. Forcing integral constraints gave an integral solution with the same objective value. The example was constructed from a Mobius strip, as the presence of a Mobius strip enforces a non-TU boundary matrix.

The feasibility region of the flat norm is given by
$$
	P = \{(x^+,x^-,y^+,y^-)\ge0:x^+ - x^- - B(y^+-y^-) = c\}
$$
where $B=[\partial_p]\in\Z^{m\times n}$. For $Z\subseteq[m]$ and $T\subseteq[n]$, denote by $B[Z,T]$ the $|Z|\times|T|$ submatrix of $B$ with rows $Z$ and columns $T$. Each vertex of $P$ is then indexed by a pair $(Z,T)$ where $B[Z,T]$ is nonsingular. The vertex is integral if and only if $c|_Z$ is in the lattice $B[Z,T]\Z^T$.

We start by reducing the problem into an $\ell^1$ problem by eliminating the $x$-variables:

**Lemma 1:** Define
$$
	F(y) = \sum_{i=1}^m w_i|c_i + (By)_i| + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^nv_j|y_j|.
$$
Then the LP value is $\min_{y\in\R^n}F(y)$ and the LP has integral optimal solution iff $F$ attains its minimum at $y\in\Z^n$.

*Proof.* Let $(x^+,x^-,y^+,y^-)$ be a feasible solution to the LP with objective $z$, set $x=x^+-x^-$ and $y=y^+-y^-$. Clearly, $F(y)=z$ as
$$
	x^+-x^--B(y^+-y^+) = c\Longrightarrow x^+-x^-=c+B(y^+-y^-),
$$
$|x_i^+-x_i^-|=x_i^++x_i^-$, and $|y_j|=y^++y^-$. Thus, the optimum $z^*$ is given by $\inf F$. $\square$

# Vertices of $P$

**Lemma 2:** A point of a standard-form polyhedron $\{u\ge0: Au=c\}$ is a vertex iff the columns of $A$ indexed by its strictly positive coordinates are linearly independent.

*Proof.* Assume that the columns $A_S$ are linearly dependent, where $S=\{j:u_j>0\}$. Thus, there is a vector $d$ with $Ad=0$ and $d_S\ne0$. Consider $u\pm\epsilon d$ for small $\epsilon>0$. Then $A(u\pm\epsilon d)=Au\pm\epsilon\cdot0=c$ and $(u\pm\epsilon d)\ge0$ for small enough $\epsilon$. Thus, we found two vectors such that
$$
	u = \frac{1}{2}(u+\epsilon d) + \frac{1}{2}(u-\epsilon d).
$$
Now assume that the columns $A_S$ are linearly independent. Let $p,q$ be feasible with $u=\lambda p +(1-\lambda)q$. Notice that for $j\in S$, $u_j=0$ forces $p_j=q_j=0$. Moreover, $Ap=Aq=c$ implies that $A(p-q)=0$. So $A_S(p-q)_S=0$. Since $A_S$ is linearly independent, we get $p-q=0$. Thus, $u=p=q$. $\square$

**Lemma 3:** A point $u\in P$ is a vertex iff $x_i^+x_i^-=0$ and $y_j^+y_j^-=0$ for all $i,j$ and the submatrix $B[Z,T]$ has full rank, where $T=\{i:y_i\ne0\}$ and $Z=\{i:x_i=0\}$. 

*Proof.* Note that the active columns (up to sign) are
$$
	\{e_i:x_i\ne0\}\cup\{B_j:y_j\ne0\}.
$$
Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be real-vectors such that
$$
	\sum_{i:x_i\ne0}\alpha_ie_i + \sum_{j:y_j\ne0}\beta_j B_j=0.
$$
Looking at rows $Z$ ($\{i:x_i=0\}$) of the above sum, we get that $B[Z,T]\beta=0$. Thus, our active columns are linearly independent iff $B[Z,T]$ has trivial kernel, i.e., full rank. Now assume that $B[Z,T]\beta=0$ for some $\beta\ne0$, then for all $i\not\in Z$ we get $\alpha_ie_i+\sum\beta_jB_j=0$ implying that our active columns are not linearly independent. Thus, $u$ is not a vertex. $\square$

**Lemma 4:** Every vertex arises as follows: choose $T\subseteq[n]$ and $Z'\subseteq[m]$ with $|Z'|=|T|$ and $\det B[Z',T]\ne0$, set
$$
\begin{align*}
	y_T = -B[Z',T]^{-1}c_{Z'} && y_{[n]\setminus T} = 0 && x = c+By
\end{align*}
$$
split into positive and negative parts. Conversely, every such choice produces a vertex of $P$.

*Proof.* Let $B[Z,T]$ have full rank, pick $|T|$ independent rows $Z'\subseteq Z$ where $Z=\{i:x_i=0\}$. To satisfy feasibility, we need $B[Z',T]y_T=-c_{Z'}$, indeed, since $x_{Z'}=0$ we get
$$
	x_{Z'} - B[Z',T]y_T = c_{Z'} \Longrightarrow
	-B[Z',T]y_T = c_{Z'}.
$$
Thus, $y_T=-B[Z',T]^{-1}c_{Z'}$.

For the converse, let $y_T=-B[Z',T]^{-1}c_{Z'}$, $y_{[n]\setminus T}=0$ and $x=c+By$ for $T\subseteq[n]$, $Z'\subseteq [m]$ with $|Z'|=|T|$. Notice that
$$
\begin{align*}
	x_{Z'} &= c_{Z'} + (By)_{Z'} \\
	&= c_{Z'} + B[Z',T]y_T \\
	&= c_{Z'} - B[Z',T]B[Z',T]^{-1}c_{Z'} \\
	&= 0.
\end{align*}
$$
$\square$

**Question:** Why are the active columns linearly independent in the converse direction? Claude just says its the same argument as before. 

Let $u$ be a vertex represented by $(Z',T)$ as in Lemma 4. Define the lattice
$$
	B[Z',T]\Z^T = \{B[Z',T]z:z\in\Z^T\}.
$$
By Lemma 4, it is clear that $u$ is integral iff $c_{Z'}\in B[Z',T]\Z^T$ as $y_T=-B[Z',T]^{-1}c_{Z'}$ and $c_{Z'}=B[Z',T]z$ for some $z\in\Z^T$. A fancier way of saying this is that the class $[c_{Z'}]$ vanishes in the finite abelian group
$$
	G(Z',T) = \Z^{Z'}/B[Z',T]\Z^T
$$
where a coset $[c]$ is given by
$$
	[c] = c + B[Z',T]\Z^T = \{c + B[Z',T]z:z\in\Z^T\}.
$$
The order of this group is $|\det B[Z',T]|$.

**Proposition 5:** Let $u$ be a vertex represented by $(Z',T)$ as in Lemma 4 with $c\in\Z^m$. Then
1. $u$ is integral iff $c_{Z'}\in B[Z',T]\Z^T$ 
2. The least common denominator of the entries of $u$ equals the order of $[c_{Z'}]$ in $G(Z',T):=\Z^{Z'}/B[Z',T]\Z^T$.
3. If $|\det B[Z',T]|=1$, then $u$ is integral.

*Proof.*
1. See paragraph above.
2. Let $k\ge1$ be the smallest integer such that $ku\in\Z^T$. Then $kc_{Z'}=B[Z',T](ky_T)\in B[Z',T]\Z^T$. The number $k$ here is the order $[c_{Z'}]$ by definition.
3. Follows from Cramer's rule:
$$
	y_{T_i} = -\frac{-\textup{adj}(A_i)}{\det B[Z',T]}
$$
where $A_i$ is $B[Z',T]$ with column $i$ replaced by $c_{Z'}$. $\square$

# Characterization Theorems

**Theorem 1:** Fix $c\in\Z^m$. The following are equivalent:
1. For every $w,v\ge0, \lambda\ge0$, the LP has an integral optimal solution;
2. every vertex of $P$ is integral;
3. for every $(Z',T)$ with $B[Z',T]$ square nonsingular, $[c|_{Z'}]=0$ in $H_{p-1}(L,L_0)\cong \Z^{Z'}/B[Z',T]Z^T$

**Theorem 2:** The following are equivalent:
1. For every $c\in\Z^m$ and all $w,v,\lambda\ge0$, the LP has an integral optimal solution;
2. $B$ is totally unimodular
3. $H_{p-1}(L,L_0)$ is torsion-free for all subcomplex pairs $L_0\subseteq L\subseteq K$.

**Theorem 3:** Fix $c\in\Z^m$. Parameterize objectives by $\theta=(w,\mu)\in\R^m_{\ge0}\times\R^n_{\ge0}$ with $\mu=\lambda v$, and for a vertex $u$ let $g(u)=(|x|,|y|)$, so the cost of $u$ is $\langle\theta,g(u)\rangle$. Then
1. The instance has an integral optimum iff $\min_{Z^n}F=\min_{\R^n}F$. In particular, if some optimal vertex has vanishing certificate $[c|_{Z'}]=0$, then an integral optimum exists.
2. Each vertex $u$ owns a closed polyhedral cone $N(u)=\{\theta:u\text{ optimal}\}$ and these finitely many cones cover the parameter space, and instances without integral optima occur only for $\theta\in\bigcup\{N(u):u\text{ fractional}\}$.
3. If $\theta$ is strictly positive and the optimal vertex $u$ is unique, then the optimal face is exactly $\{u\}$, and the instance has an integral optimum iff $[c|_{Z'}]=0$ for $u$'s data.

# The Counterexample

Claude also discussed the counterexample from [[2026-07-09]]. Recall that our fractional optimum was
$$
\begin{align*}
	z &= 3.00009 \\
	\mathbf{x} &= \frac{1}{2}[3,4] + \frac{1}{2}[0,1] + \frac{1}{2}[1,2] + \frac{1}{2}[4,5] + \frac{1}{2}[2,3] - \frac{1}{2}[0,5] \\
	\mathbf{y} &= \frac{1}{2}[3,4,6] + [0,4,6] - \frac{1}{2}[0,3,6] - \frac{1}{2}[1,4,6] - \frac{1}{2}[0,1,6] - [4,6,7] \\
	&\phantom{=}+ \frac{1}{2}[1,4,7] - [2,4,7] - \frac{1}{2}[1,2,7] + \frac{1}{2}[4,5,7] - \frac{1}{2}[5,7,8] - \frac{1}{2}[2,7,8] \\
	&\phantom{=}- \frac{1}{2}[2,3,8] - \frac{1}{2}[0,5,8] - \frac{1}{2}[0,3,8]
\end{align*}
$$
In the language above, $y^*$ is supported on 15 triangles $T$ and $x^*$ vanishes on 15 edges $Z'$:
$$
\begin{align*}
	T &= \{[3,4,6], [0,4,6] ,[0,3,6],[1,4,6],[0,1,6],[4,6,7],[1,4,7],[2,4,7],[1,2,7],[4,5,7],[5,7,8], \\
	&\phantom{=\{}[2,7,8],[2,3,8],[0,5,8],[0,3,8]\} \\
	Z' &= \text{edges} \setminus \{[3,4],[0,1],[1,2],[4,5],[2,3],[0,5]\}.
\end{align*}
$$
Our group corresponds to $\Z^{Z'}/B[Z',T]\Z^T\cong Z/2$ as $k=2$ is the smallest positive integer such that $kc_{Z'}\in B[Z',T]\Z^T$.

However, we did find that this solution tied with an integral solution. Consider our two solutions $u_\textup{int}$ and $u_\textup{frac}$. According to Theorem 3, there are two closed polyhedral cones:
$$
\begin{align*}
	N(u_\textup{int}) &= \{\theta:u_\textup{int} \text{ optimal}\} \\
	N(u_\textup{frac}) &= \{\theta:u_\textup{frac} \text{ optimal}\}.
\end{align*}
$$
Our example showed that $N(u_\textup{int})\cap N(u_\textup{frac})\ne\emptyset$. Theoretically, one could find a perturbation $\mathbf{\epsilon}$ such that $\theta+\epsilon\in N(u_\textup{int})$, that is, $\mathbf{\epsilon}$ points in the direction of $N(u_\text{int})$, but is small enough that it stays in the closed polyhedral cone. 

The direction should be straightforward: if $\theta$ is sitting on a face of $N(u_\textup{frac})$, then perturb $\theta$ normal to the face. However, this needs to be done without computing $N(u_\textup{frac})$. The perturbation would also have to be done in a manner that does not change the objective. Hence, we would have to somehow extract $u_\textup{int}$.
