I spent some time exploring the parameter space for the example given in [[2026-07-09]]. More specifically, I modified the weights of triangles as follows:
$$
	v_i = \begin{cases}
		\theta_1 & \text{ if $y_i$ is not a Mobius triangle} \\
		\theta_2 & \text{ if $y_i$ is a Mobius triangle.}
	\end{cases}
$$
I then did an exhaustive computation of $(\theta_1,\theta_2)\in[0.1,3]\times[0.1,3]$ with a step size of $\Delta\theta_i=0.01$. For each set of weights, I solved the flat norm LP and stored the objective value $z^*$, and whether $\mathbf{y}^*$ is integral. In other words, I looked at the following map:
$$
	f(\theta_1,\theta_2) = \begin{cases}
		(z^*, 1) & \text{if }\mathbf{y}^*\in\Z^n \\
		(z^*, 0) & \text{if }\mathbf{y}^*\not\in\Z^n.
	\end{cases}
$$
I then plotted this map using a contour plot:

![[SS_2026-07-22_1784757958.png]]

We find the following interesting facts:
- Every fractional solution $(z^*,\mathbf{x}^*,\mathbf{y}^*)$ has a corresponding integral solution with the same objective $z^*$.
- For each objective value $z^*$, its corresponding parameter space is divided into two parts: fractional vertices and integral vertices.

My idea is as follows:
1. Compute the flat norm LP
2. If the solution is fractional, tweak the weights so that we get an equivalent integral solution.

# Parameter Space and It's Tangent Space

This strategy requires us to have a strong understanding of the parameter space. That is, fix $K$ and $c$ and look at the space $\theta=(\mathbf{w},\lambda\mathbf{v})$. Assume that the LP gave $z=3.00009$ as in our example. For any vertex of the LP $u=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})$, consider $g(u)=(\mathbf{x}^++\mathbf{x}^-,\mathbf{y}^++\mathbf{y}^-)$. Then our objective is given by
$$
	z(\mathbf{\theta},u) = \langle\theta,g(u)\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_ig(u)_i.
$$
We want to look at the following hypersurface in $\R_{\ge0}^{2N-1}$ where $N=m+n$:
$$
\begin{align*}
	\cal{S}_{z^*} &= \{\theta\ge0,u\ge0:z(\theta,u)=z^*\} \\
	&= \{\theta\ge0,u\ge0:\sum_{i=1}^N\theta_i(g(u))_i=z^*\}.
\end{align*}
$$
Notice that the map $z:\R^{2N}\rightarrow\R$ is smooth and $\nabla_\theta z=g(u)$ on $\cal{S}$. Experimentation suggests that $\nabla_\theta z$ is orthogonal to the direction we need to travel, i.e., we need to nudge $\theta$ tangent to $\nabla_\theta z$:

**Gradient $\nabla_\theta z$ projected to $(w1,w2)$ space:**

**Tangent $(v,-u)$ of the gradient:**

In our higher dimension, we want to look at the tangent space:
$$
	T_\theta = \{v\in\R^N:g(u)^\top v = 0\}.
$$
However, there are a ton of options to choose from! In the 2D picture we took
$$
	v = e_1 - \frac{e_1^\top g(u)}{g(u)^\top g(u)}g(u) \in T_\theta.
$$
If we want an orthonormal basis for $T_\theta$, we could use a [Householder reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Householder_transformation) and compute the QR decomposition of $g(u)$ (viewed as a $N\times1$ matrix). Set $\hat{g}=g(u)/\|g(u)\|$ and $\alpha=\textup{sgn}(g_1)$. Then the QR decomposition is given by
$$
	Q = I - 2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top~\text{ where }~\mathbf{u} = \frac{\hat{g}-\alpha e_1}{\|\hat{g}-\alpha e_1\|}.
$$
Then for any $\mathbf{\gamma}\in\R^{N-1}$, 
$$
	g(u)^\top\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\gamma_iQ_{i+1}\right)=0,
$$
i.e., the columns $2,\dots,N$ of $Q$ form a basis for $T_\theta$. 

*Proof.* Denote by $G=\|\hat{g}-\alpha e_1\|$. Notice that for $j\ge2$,
$$
	u_j = \frac{(\hat{g}-\alpha e_1)_j}{G} = \frac{\hat{g}_j-\alpha (e_1)_j}{G} = \frac{\hat{g}_j}{G}
$$
and
$$
	G^2 = (\hat{g}-\alpha e_1)^\top(\hat{g}-\alpha e_1) = \hat{g}^\top\hat{g}-\alpha\hat{g}^\top e_1-\alpha e_1^\top\hat{g} + \alpha^2e_1^\top e_1 = 1 - 2\alpha\hat{g}_1+\alpha^2 = 2(1-\alpha\hat{g}_1)
$$
as $\alpha^2=1$. Thus,
$$
\begin{align*}
	\hat{g}^\top(Qe_j) &= \hat{g}^\top(e_j - 2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^\top e_j) \\
	&= \hat{g}^\top\left[e_j - 2(\mathbf{u}^\top e_j)\mathbf{u}\right] \\
	&= \hat{g}^\top\left[e_j - 2u_j\mathbf{u}\right] \\
	&= \hat{g}_j - 2u_j\hat{g}^\top\mathbf{u} \\
	&= \hat{g}_j - 2\left(\frac{\hat{g}_j}{G}\right)\left(\frac{\hat{g}^\top(\hat{g}-\alpha e_1)}{G}\right) \\
	&= \hat{g}_j - 2\left(\frac{\hat{g}_j}{G}\right)\left(\frac{\hat{g}^\top\hat{g}-\alpha\hat{g}^\top e_1)}{G}\right) \\
	&= \hat{g}_j - 2\left(\frac{\hat{g}_j}{G}\right)\left(\frac{1-\alpha\hat{g}_1}{G}\right) \\
	&= \hat{g}_j - \frac{2\hat{g}_j(1-\alpha\hat{g}_1)}{G^2} \\
	&= \hat{g}_j - \frac{2\hat{g}_j(1-\alpha\hat{g}_1)}{2(1-\alpha\hat{g}_1)} \\
	&= 0.
\end{align*} 
$$
Orthonormal is left as an exercise. $\square$

While we can compute an orthonormal basis for $T_\theta$, it is unclear which tangent we should take. However, if we could find the "best" one, then we would have an algorithm to find an integral solution given a fractional.

# The Algorithm

Denote the tangent to $\nabla_\theta z=g(u)$ by $\tau(u)$. One approach to this is as follows:
1. Set $\theta_0$ by the given parameters.
2. Solve the flat norm LP to find $u_0$, record the objective value $z^*$.
3. At step $k$, do the following:
	1. If $u_k\in\Z^N$, stop.
	2. Otherwise, set $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k + \rho_k \tau(u_k)$
	3. Find $u_{k+1}$ such that $(\theta_{k+1},u_{k+1})\in\cal{S}_{z^*}$.

The difficult step is projecting back into $\cal{S}_{z^*}$. Let $u_{k+1}=\mu u_k$. Notice that
$$
\begin{align*}
	z^* &= z(\theta_{k+1},u_{k+1})  \\
	&= \langle\theta_{k+1}, \mu u_k\rangle \\
	&= \mu\langle\theta_{k+1},u_k\rangle
\end{align*}
$$
implies that
$$
	\mu = \frac{z^*}{\langle\theta_{k+1},u_k\rangle}.
$$
So, we get back to $\cal{S}_*$ by setting
$$
	u_{k+1} = \frac{z^*}{\langle\theta_{k+1},u_k\rangle}u_k.
$$

However, this doesn't respect the homology constraints. Notice that
$$
\begin{align*}
	\mu(\mathbf{x}^+-\mathbf{x^-})-\mu B(\mathbf{y}^+-\mathbf{y}^-) &= \mu\mathbf{c} \ne \mathbf{c}.
\end{align*}
$$

Notice that if we set $\mathbf{u}_{k+1}=(\mathbf{x}+\epsilon,\mathbf{y}+\delta)$, then
$$
	\langle \theta, (\mathbf{x}+\epsilon,\mathbf{y}+\delta)\rangle = \langle \theta, \mathbf{u}\rangle + \langle\theta,(\epsilon,\delta)\rangle = z^* + \langle\theta,(\epsilon,\delta)\rangle.
$$
What we need to do, is solve the following LP:
$$
\begin{align*}
	&\textup{minimize} & \|\epsilon\| + \|\delta\| \\
	&\textup{subject to} & \langle\theta_{k+1},(\epsilon,\delta)\rangle = 0 \\
	&& (\mathbf{x} + \epsilon) - B(\mathbf{y} + \delta) = \mathbf{c}\\
	&& \mathbf{\epsilon},\delta\in\R^N.
\end{align*}
$$

Call this problem $\cal{P}2$ and the flat norm $\cal{P}1$. Then our algorithm is as follows:
1. Set $\theta_0$ by the given parameters.
2. Solve $\cal{P}1$ to find $u_0$, record the objective value $z^*$.
3. At step $k$, do the following:
	1. If $u_k\in\Z^N$, stop.
	2. Otherwise, set $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k + \rho_k \tau(u_k)$
	3. Find $u_{k+1}$ by solving $\cal{P}2$.
4. Compute $z(\theta,u\rangle$ with the original parameters $\theta_0$.

However, I don't know if this will converge in a linear number of steps. We would likely need a good step size to ensure it doesn't overshoot. It would also be great if we could quickly solve $\cal{P}2$.

---

**Update 7/24/26:** I implemented this approach with the exception that I find $u_{k+1}$ by solving the flat norm problem with the new weights instead of solving $\cal{P}2$. The algorithm converged to an integral solution. Moreover, the solution was the **exact** integral solution! For a step size, I used
$$
	\rho_k = \frac{1}{(k+1)\|g(u)\|^2}.
$$
