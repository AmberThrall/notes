We know that the existence of two disjoint Mobius strip results in absolute determinant $\ge4$. We are unsure of the existence of two overlapping Mobius strips. Let's explore $\R P^2$ which contains multiple overalapping Mobius strips, but is constructed form a single Mobius strip.

Below is the triangulation of $\R P^2$ we will look at:

![[SS_2026-09-18_1789765283.png]]

There are three Mobius strips:
$$
\begin{align*}
	M_1 &= \{[0,1,3], [0,3,5], [3,4,5], [1,4,5], [0,1,4]\} \\
	M_2 &= \{[1,2,3], [2,3,4], [3,4,5], [1,4,5], [1,2,5]\} \\
	M_3 &= \{[0,2,4], [2,3,4], [3,4,5], [0,3,5], [0,2,5]\}.
\end{align*}
$$
We start by looking at the submatrix corresponding to $L=M_1\cup M_2$.
$$
	B(M_1\cup M_2) = \begin{array}{c|ccc} 
		& [0,1,3] & [0,3,5] & [3,4,5] & [1,4,5] & [0,1,4] & [1,2,3] & [2,3,4] & [1,2,5] \\ \hline

I wrote code (see below) which computed every $k\times k$ minor for $1\le k\le 10$ and found that all minors had absolute determinant at most 2, i.e., $B$ is totally bimodular!

```python
m, n = A.shape
num_bad_minors = 0

for k in range(1, min(m, n) + 1):
    print("Testing all {}x{} minors...".format(k, k))
    for rows in combinations(range(m), k):
        for cols in combinations(range(n), k):
            submatrix = A[np.ix_(rows, cols)]
            det = np.linalg.det(submatrix)
            if np.abs(det) > 2:
                num_bad_minors += 1
                print("{}".format(det))

print("Found {} minors with absolute determinant greater than 2.".format(num_bad_minors))
```

Subdividing this triangulation causes the number of minors to blow up. Restricting to just two Mobius strips and including all triangles in our submatrix resulted in determiant 0, $\pm1$, or $\pm 2$.

**Theorem:** Let $[\partial_2]$ be the $m\times n$ boundary matrix for a triangulation of $\R P^2$. Then every $n\times n$ submatrix of $[\partial_2]$ has absolute determinant at most 2. Moreover, any square submatrix of $[\partial_2]$ has absolute determinant at most $2^k$ for some $k$.

*Proof.* Let $A$ be a subcomplex of $\R P^2$. The snake lemma gives that
$$
	H_1(A)\rightarrow H_1(\R P^2)\rightarrow H_1(\R P^2,A)\rightarrow H_0(A)\rightarrow H_0(\R P^2)
$$
is an exact sequence. Recall that
$$
	H_k(\R P^2) = \begin{cases}
		\Z & k = 0 \\
		\Z/2\Z & k = 1 \\
		0 & k\ge 2
	\end{cases}
$$
so
$$
	H_1(A)\xrightarrow{i_*} \Z/2\Z\xrightarrow{\pi_*} H_1(\R P^2,A)\xrightarrow{\partial} H_0(A)\xrightarrow{j_*} \Z.
$$
Because the sequence is exact, $\textup{im}\partial=\textup{ker} j_*$. Note that $j_*$ is induced by the inclusion $j:A\hookrightarrow\R P^2$ so it follows that for a vertex $a_i\in A$, $j_*[a_i]=[a_i]$. Assume that we have two vertices $a_1$ and $a_2$ which get mapped to $[a_1]$ and $[a_2]$ in $H_0(\R P^2)$. Because $\R P^2$ is connected, it follows that there is a 1-chain $\gamma$ such that $\partial_1\gamma = a_2-a_1$ in $\R P^2$. In other words, $j_*(a_2-a_1)=0$ implying that $[a_1]=[a_2]$. Thus, every class in $H_0(A)$ gets mapped to the same generator. This implies that $\rank(j_*)=1$ which results in $\text{ker}(j_*)\cong\Z^{\beta_0-1}$ where $\beta_0$ is the number of connected components of $A$. Hence, we can reduce our exact sequence to
$$
	H_1(A)\xrightarrow{i_*} \Z/2\Z\xrightarrow{\pi_*} H_1(\R P^2,A)\xrightarrow{\partial} \Z^{\beta_0-1}\rightarrow0.
$$
Due to exactness at $\Z/2\Z$, it follows that $\text{ker}\pi_*=\text{im} i_*$. By the first isomorphism theorem,
$$
	\text{im}\pi_*\cong (\Z/2\Z)/\text{ker}(\pi_*) = (\Z/2\Z)/\text{im}(i_*) = \text{coker}(i_*).
$$
We also have exactness at $H_1(\R P^2,A)$ which implies that $\text{ker}\partial=\text{im}\pi_*\cong\text{coker}(i_*)$. So we can reduce our exact sequence further to
$$
	0\rightarrow\text{coker}(i_*)\rightarrow H_1(\R P^2,A)\xrightarrow{\partial}\Z^{\beta_0-1}\rightarrow0.
$$
This sequence splits as $\Z^{\beta_0-1}$ is a free abelian group, thus it follows that
$$
	H_1(\R P^2,A)\cong \text{coker}(i_*)\oplus \Z^{c-1},
$$
i.e., either $H_1(\R P^2,A)$ is torsion free, or its only torsion subgroup is $\Z/2\Z$. Indeed, because $i_*:H_1(A)\rightarrow\Z/2\Z$ it follows that $i_*$ is onto or $i_*$ is trivial. These two cases give $\text{coker}(i_*)=0$ and $\text{coker}(i_*)=\Z/2\Z$, respectively. Therefore, every $n\times n$ minor, where $n$ is the number of columns, has absolute determinant at most 2. 

Now let $L_0\subset L\subset\R P^2$ be a pair of pure subcomplexes of dimension 1 and 2, respectively. Let $p: S^2\rightarrow\R P^2$ be the usual sphere construction where a point $p$ is identified with its antipodal point $-p$. Set $L'=p^{-1}(L)$ and $L_0'=p^{-1}(L_0)$. Because $L\neq\R P^2$, it follows that $L'\subseteq S^2\setminus\{p\}$ for some point $p$. Since $S^2\setminus\{p\}\cong\R^2$, it follows that $L'$ is embeddable in $\R^2$. Thus it follows that $H_1(L',L_0)$ must be torsion-free as the boundary map $[\partial_2]$ for any complex embedded in $\R^2$ is TU. Notice that our map $p$ gives a lifted triangulation of $S^2$. Define $t:C_2(L)\rightarrow C_2(L')$ by 
$$
	t(\sigma) = \tau + \tau'
$$
where $\tau$ and $\tau'$ are the lifted triangles of $p^{-1}(\sigma)$. Then it follows that $t(C_2(L_0))=C_2(L_0')$ implying that $t$ induces a homomorphism $t_*:H_1(L,L_0)\rightarrow H_1(L',L_0')$. Because $p$ induces a map $p_\#:C_1(L')\rightarrow C_1(L)$ given by
$$
	p_\#\left(\sum_ic_i\sigma_i\right) = \sum_ic_ip(\sigma_i)
$$
it follows that
$$
	p_\#(t(\sigma)) = p_\#(\tau + \tau') = p_\#(\tau)+p_\#(\tau') = 2\sigma
$$
implying that $p_*\circ t_*=2$ where $p_*:H_1(L',L_0')\rightarrow H_1(L,L_0)$ is induced by $p_\#$. Let $x\in H_1(L,L_0)$ be such that $kx=0$ for some $k\in\Z$. Notice that
$$
	kt_*(x) = t_*(kx) = t_*(0) = 0	
$$
implying that $t_*(x)$ is torsion in $H_1(L',L_0')$. But $H_1(L',L_0')$ is torsion free, so we get that $t_*(x)=0$ which implies that 
$$
	2x = p_*(t_*(x)) = p_*(0) = 0.
$$
Therefore, the torsion subgroup of $H_1(L,L_0)$ is $(Z/2\Z)^k$ for some $k$. $\square$
